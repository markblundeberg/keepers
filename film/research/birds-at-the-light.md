# Birds at the light: what the keepers and the literature say

For the film's birds kit (owner C, `film/engine/birds.js`, `birds.glsl`). Written 28 Oct 2026.

Status marks on every point:

- **[R]** read in the BAAS reports (chunk id in `data/chunks/*.jsonl`; quotes are verbatim).
- **[L-full]** read in the full text of a published work; **[L-abs]** read only in an abstract, a first page or a secondary summary (so the claim is the summary's, not checked against the paper).
- **[M]** from my own general knowledge, not checked against a source this session.
- **[I]** inferred or invented for the film. The kit's choices that rest on these are listed at the end.

## 1. The night itself (South Bishop, 21-22 Oct 1887)

- [R 1887-00167] "hundreds of birds of all sorts flying around the lantern from 11 P.M. to 2.30 A.M. (22d); 272 killed, *viz.*, 'Larks,' 146; Starlings, 64; Blackbirds, 38 (caught three old males); Thrushes, 24. Several birds seen on the rock at daylight with broken wings. (S.E., 4; O. M.)"
  - Proportions of the dead: Larks 53.7%, Starlings 23.5%, Blackbirds 14.0%, Thrushes 8.8%.
  - "seen on the rock at daylight with broken wings": as printed, these birds were *alive* at daylight (seen, with broken wings), and on the rock, not the gallery. The 272 are "killed"; the entry doesn't say where they were picked up.
  - "caught three old males": the keepers took live Blackbirds at the lantern. Whether the three are inside the 38 isn't stated.
- [R 1880-00215] The committee in 1880: "South Bishop the most deadly station."
- [R 1887-00168] Next day's entry: "*South Bishop* L.H. (see entry under yesterday)." Same night: Godrevy "Starlings, Larks, Blackbirds, and Thrushes; a good many killed and caught. (Light, misty.)"; Eddystone "an important movement from 10 P.M. (21st) until 4 A.M."
- [R 1884-00221] "Mr. Hills, of the South Bishop, says that flocks of Larks pass south, during the daytime..." (Larks are mostly day migrants there; that night they were at the lantern after dark.)

## 2. When birds come to a light

- **Dark, overcast, misty or rainy nights; not clear or moonlit ones.**
  - [R 1879-00052] "When the nights are dark and cloudy, no stars appearing, in rain, fogs and snowstorms, flocks of birds during the night migrations will crowd round the lanterns... On these nights birds will often remain for hours in the vicinity of a light, circling round and round... at the first break in the clouds, the stars becoming visible, or the first streak of early dawn, they will resume their flight to the nearest land."
  - [R 1882-00083] "It is only on dark rainy nights, snow or fog, that these casualties occur; when the nights are light, or any stars visible, the birds appear to give the lanterns a wide berth."
  - [R 1882-00080] "On clear moonlight nights nothing at all is caught or found."
  - [L-full] Eagle Clarke (1912) vol. I pp. 284-285 (Eddystone, 1901): "The lantern of a light-station, I discovered, is simply a decoy... which would only 'work' under peculiar conditions, which are dependent upon the amount of moisture (rain, haze, cloud) present in the atmosphere." On 15-16 Oct 1901, as the sky alternated between clear and overcast: "The beams grew conspicuous when the sky became overcast... and then the birds immediately approached the beacon; but as soon as this condition passed away, the rays at once thinned down... the birds sheared off" (p. 295).
  - [L-abs] Gauthreaux & Belser (2006), first page only: lights attract migrants "particularly when the sky is cloudy and the ceiling is low"; Romanes (1883) compared it to insects at a flame.
  - [L-abs] Larkin & Frase (1988), *J. Comp. Psychol.* 102:90-93 (abstract via search summary): radar-tracked night migrants flew arcs or circles centred on a lit 308 m tower, radii > 100 m, **only while low cloud surrounded the tower**; none in clear skies.
  - [L-abs] Rebke et al. (2019), *Biol. Conserv.* 233:220-227 (abstract): with stars hidden, more birds came to continuous light than to blinking light; red continuous light drew fewer than white, green or blue.
  - [L-abs] Hüppop et al. (2016), *Bird Study* (summary): mass collisions at the lit FINO1 platform in the German Bight coincided with worsening weather and high nocturnal call rates; songbirds most involved.
- **Fixed lights drew more than revolving or flashing ones.** The South Bishop's light was a revolving one, yet the most deadly station.
  - [R 1884-00176] "Revolving lights, whatever the height, yield fewer returns than fixed, but are better than flash or red sectors."
  - [R 1882-00123] Barrels Rock L.V.: "a very quick red light... no birds ever struck... I believe the quick revolution frightens them away."
  - [L-abs] Jones & Francis (2003), *J. Avian Biol.* 34:328-333 (abstract): Long Point lighthouse kills fell from ~393 per autumn to ~18.5 per year after the beam was made narrower and less powerful in 1989.
- **Low, isolated lights kill most.** [R 1880-00034] lightships and isolated rocks such as Bell Rock or Skerryvore show the most deaths, "a great preponderance above such stations as Cape Wrath (which is 400 feet above the sea)." The South Bishop's light is about 44 m above the sea (CONTRACT.md).

## 3. What they do there

- **Circle, for hours, in numbers.** [R 1879-00052, 1880-00118] "lost birds will wheel for hours round a lightship." [R 1882-00005] "night after night hundreds of thousands of small birds circled round the lanterns".
- **Direction of circling: "against the sun", once.** [R 1882-00005] At the Isle of May the birds circled "flying 'against the sun'". "Against the sun" (widdershins) is anticlockwise seen from above. This is the only statement of direction I found in nine years of reports; Agnew's light was **fixed**. No modern source I read states a preferred direction. [I] The kit gives most birds an anticlockwise drift and some the other way, with many crossing at all angles (next point).
- **Not a tidy vortex: fluttering in, and crossing the beams at all angles.** [L-full] Eagle Clarke (1912) I pp. 290-291, Eddystone, 12-13 Oct 1901 (the night of the painting): "Hosts of glittering objects... were fluttering in, or crossing at all angles, the brilliant revolving beams of light... Those which simply crossed the rays were illumined for a moment only, and became mere spectres on passing into the gloom beyond... above and below the madding crowd in the illumined zone, great numbers of the migrants flitted around in all directions in the semi-darkness."
- **Birds stay longer where it's lit.** [L-abs] Van Doren et al. (2017), *PNAS* 114:11175-11180 (summaries; the paper's page wouldn't load): birds in the Tribute in Light beams slowed, circled and called more; densities up to 60-150 times baseline; they dispersed within minutes of the lights going off. [L-full] Clarke I p. 289: "Most of the emigrants went steadily southwards, but many, dazzled by the light, tarried."
- **A steady stream with fresh arrivals; densities rise and fall in pulses.** [R 1885-00020] Bell Rock, 16 Oct 1884: "Birds began to arrive at 7.30 P.M., striking lightly and flying off again... Numbers went on increasing till midnight, when it seemed that a vast flock had arrived, as they now swarmed in the rays of light... At 3 A.M. another flock seemed to have arrived... All birds went off at 6 A.M." [L-full] Clarke I p. 290: "the continual arrival, down to almost its very close, of fresh emigrants."
- **What the eye sees.** [R 1882-00083] Galloper L.V.: "Thousands of birds were flying round the lantern from 11.30 p.m. to 4.45 a.m., their white breasts, as they dashed to and fro in the circle of light, having the appearance of a heavy fall of snow" (a large share were Larks). [L-full] Clarke I p. 286: "those illumined by the slowly revolving beams from the lantern had the appearance of brilliant glittering objects"; "even the conspicuous spots on the breasts of the Song-Thrushes were entirely effaced... by the dazzling brilliancy of the light"; "the birds careering around became mere apparitions on passing from the rays into the semi-darkness beyond." This is the painting's logic: pale in the beam, gone outside it.
- **They call.** [L-full] Clarke I p. 291: "The babel of tongues... not the cries of enchantment, but of surprise and alarm... the loud rattling notes of the Blackbird... Some Skylarks every now and then... broke out into a few notes of song." [R 1887-00185] Nash, Lapwings: "They were very noisy, and kept up quite a concert." (For the sound designer.)

## 4. How strikes happen

- **Up the beam.** [L-full] Clarke I pp. 286-287 (23 Sept 1901): "The birds which struck the lantern did so after travelling directly up the beams of light; but a number of them flew high and passed over the dome." pp. 290-291: "Those which winged their way up the beams towards the lantern were innumerable, and resembled streaks of approaching light. These either struck the glass, or, recovering themselves, passed out of the ray ere the fatal focal point was reached."
- **Lightly or hard.** [R 1880-00006] Bell Rock: "many struck; some lightly, and flew away again; others harder, and were seen fluttering away into the darkness." [R 1885-00020] "striking lightly and flying off again"; later "striking hard, fell dead on balcony, or rebounded off and went over the rail, falling into the sea."
- **Against the glass, the framing and the dome.** [L-full] Clarke I p. 291: "Some of those that struck fell like stones... others glanced off more or less injured or stunned, to perish miserably in the surf below. Others, again, beat violently against the windows... Many... came in sharp contact with the copper dome of the tower, making it resound like a drum." [R 1885-00020] "all kinds crowded on to the lantern windows, trying to force their way to the light. The noise they made shrieking and battering the windows... baffles description."
- **On the windward side, with the wind.**
  - [R 1880-00215] Bardsey: "birds strike in general with the wind; without, very rare exception." Wolf Rock: "when they strike the lantern it is generally N.W."
  - [R 1884-00030] Isle of May: "All birds striking on S.E. side, with the wind."
  - [R 1882-00128] Samphire Island: "Almost all birds strike with S.E. winds, and generally on the east side of the lantern."
  - [R 1887-00176] Eddystone: "killed, striking windward side of lantern."
  - [R 1885-00025] Isle of May: Woodcocks killed on the S.E. side and the others on the N.W. side the same night, read by Agnew as two streams flying opposite ways.
  - [R 1881-00137] Skerries: "Many birds are killed in strong winds, as they then fly with greater force."
  - [I] For the South Bishop that night (wind S.E. 4) the kit puts most strikes on the S.E. quarter of the lantern.
- **Species differ: the four of the count.** [L-full] Clarke I pp. 292-293, Eddystone 12-13 Oct 1901:
  - **Starling**: "the most susceptible subject present... hustled past us... to reach the lantern, and, being baulked on the threshold by the windows, made vigorous attempts to reach the seductive lamp. After having exhausted itself... it sat on the sills and sashes, drinking in, as it were, the light, until it became quite stupefied."
  - **Skylark**: "came up in great numbers to the light, but not being accustomed to perch on such slight coigns of vantage as the metal framework of the windows, it fluttered violently against the glass for a time, and, becoming exhausted, sank prostrate on the gallery."
  - **Blackbird and Song Thrush**: "approached the lantern more freely than their congeners, but they had a habit of coming up to some extent 'side on,' so to speak, and consequently they glanced off either a little stunned or quite uninjured. These birds did not attempt to remain at the lantern."
  - The Eddystone dead that night (those that "did not fall overboard"): 76 Skylarks, 53 Starlings, 17 Blackbirds, 9 Song Thrushes. That is 49 / 34 / 11 / 6% against South Bishop's 54 / 24 / 14 / 9% fourteen years earlier: the same order, larks first.
- **Sea-birds rarely strike; Woodcock die outright.** [R 1884-00240] (Not our species; kept for movement II.)

## 5. Where the dead and the stunned end up

- **On the gallery (the "balcony")**: [R 1885-00020] "fell dead on balcony"; [R 1881-00132] "each on striking would drop into the gallery and remain till daylight, when, if not too much injured, they would fly".
- **Over the rail into the sea**, uncounted: [R 1885-00020, 1879-00020] "rebounding off, fell into the sea"; [R 1881-00148] Tuskar: "twelve hundred killed in one night, and hundreds more fell into the sea that we did not get"; [R 1887-00142] Eddystone: "many striking the lantern and lost over". [L-full] Clarke I p. 292 footnote: he hung netting from the gallery railing "to prevent any birds that struck from falling over."
- **At the foot of the tower and on the rock**: [R 1887-00177] Bull Point: "two Thrushes found dead at foot of tower at daylight"; [R 1887-00012] Pentland Skerries: "one Starling found dead at foot of tower"; [R 1887-00243] Fastnet: "a Skylark and Twite (supposed) found dead on rock, 9 A.M."
- **Resting on sills and sashes until dawn**: [R 1880-00028] "A number seen asleep all night on window-sashes; left at first streak of dawn." [R 1879-00019] "rest on the window-sills of the tower and the balconies, or endeavour to obtain entrance to the tower. Whenever dawn appears they resume their flight." [L-full] Clarke I p. 290: "At daybreak, all, save a few Starlings resting in a dazed condition in the recesses of the windows, had passed away."
- **Picked up and counted.** The keepers counted by species (the printed line) and posted legs and wings "in the envelopes provided for this purpose" (committee's request, 1886 report, per TREATMENT.md). [L-full] Clarke I p. 294: at the Eddystone the dead Thrushes and Skylarks "were served up at dinner for several days." (True and plain; not for the film.)

## 6. The four species: size, shape, flight

Speeds and biometry from Alerstam et al. (2007) *PLoS Biol.* 5:e197, Protocol S1 [L-full; the table itself]: Ue is the mean equivalent airspeed of radar-tracked birds in continuous flapping flight; • rows are Bruderer & Boldt (2001).

| species | Ue (m/s), Lund / • B&B | mass (kg) | span (m) | wing area (m²) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Skylark *Alauda arvensis* | 15.1 (n=15) / 12.7 (n=20) | 0.039 | 0.35 | 0.0207 |
| Starling *Sturnus vulgaris* | 16.2 (n=4) / 12.4 (n=9) | 0.083 | 0.38 | 0.0244 |
| Song Thrush *Turdus philomelos* | 11.0 (n=6) / 11.7 (n=8) | 0.068 | 0.36 | 0.0218 |
| Blackbird *Turdus merula* | not in the table | ~0.10 [M] | ~0.36 [M] | ~0.030 [I] |

- **Wingbeat frequency**: [I, computed] Pennycuick's (1990) cruising-flight allometry f = m^(3/8) g^(1/2) b^(-23/24) S^(-1/3) ρ^(-3/8), with the masses and areas above: Skylark 8.6 Hz, Starling 10.0 Hz, Song Thrush 10.1 Hz, Blackbird 10.2 Hz. The formula is recalled from memory [M]: I couldn't open the original (the publisher's site refused the download), and a secondary quotation of the 1996 revision gives a different span exponent (-17/24 with a wing-inertia term). [L-abs] Bruderer et al. (2010) *Ibis* 152:272-291 give measured radar wingbeat frequencies for 153 species, but I couldn't get its values. Treat the numbers as about 8-12 Hz, not as measured. Birds hovering or fluttering slowly in a beam probably beat faster than in cruising [I].
- **Silhouettes and flight style** [M, field-guide knowledge, not checked this session]:
  - *Starling*: ~21 cm; short square tail; pointed triangular wings (the "flying cross"); fast, direct, with short glides. Autumn adults are dark, heavily spangled pale; a darkish bird.
  - *Skylark*: ~18 cm; broad, rather rounded wings with a pale trailing edge; medium tail with white outer feathers; fluttery flight, slightly undulating. Pale buff-white underparts (the Galloper's "white breasts... heavy fall of snow").
  - *Blackbird*: ~25 cm; the longest tail of the four; rounded wings; flight with bursts of flapping and short closed-wing glides. Males all black, females dark brown.
  - *Song Thrush*: ~23 cm; medium tail; flight direct with brief pauses (flap-bounding); pale spotted underparts, warm buff underwing.
- **Size on screen**: a 0.35 m wingspan at 30 m with the contract's 1080p frame and a ~40° vertical field of view is about 12 px [I, arithmetic]. Most flying birds will be specks a few to 30 px across, which is how the painting draws them.

## 7. Scale of the night [I, arithmetic from the printed line]

- 272 killed in 3.5 hours is 78 an hour on average, about one every 46 s. At a peak of two to three times the mean (an assumption), about one death every 20 s.
- Strikes outnumbered deaths ("striking lightly and flying off again", "a good number struck, and ... killed", 1887-00176), but no South Bishop ratio is printed. The kit assumes 2.5 non-fatal strikes per death, plus birds lost over the rail (uncounted) at 0.4 per counted death. Both ratios are invented.
- So at the true rate a 6 s shot at the height of the night sees on average about one strike, and a death every third or fourth such shot. The kit follows each shot's clock (`shot.hour`); a shot that runs the clock faster than real time gets strikes faster in proportion (REQUESTS.md, from C).
- "Hundreds of birds of all sorts flying around the lantern": the kit's peak is about 600 birds within ~60 m of the lantern at once, at about 1 A.M. [I: "hundreds" is the record; the shape of the night (rise from 11 P.M., peak, thinning to 2.30) follows the Bell Rock and Eddystone diaries, not anything printed for South Bishop].
