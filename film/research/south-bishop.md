# The South Bishop lighthouse as it was in 1887

Research for the film's light kit (owner B), 28 Oct 2026. Every fact is marked:

- **[seen]**: I looked at the primary page or image myself (scan, photograph).
- **[read]**: read in a secondary source or in OCR text of a primary source whose page image I didn't view.
- **[inferred]**: worked out from the above; the working is given.
- **[chosen]**: nothing found; a plain choice made for the film.

## The light

- **Character in 1887: "One rev. br. lt., 20 secs."** [seen, F87]. Same in 1879 ("One br. rev. lt. 20 secs.", F79 [read]) and 1884 ("White, revolving, 20 seconds interval", E84 [read]). So a white flash every 20 s, the same rhythm for nine years.
- **Height of the flame above high water: 144 ft = 43.9 m** [seen, F87]. Findlay: "the height of the flame in feet above the highest tide level, consequently it is its minimum height" [read, F79/F87 explanation].
- **Range: 18 miles** in clear weather from 10 ft above the sea [seen, F87; definition read].
- **Established 1839** [seen, F87].
- **Apparatus code "1b"** [seen, F87]. The key: "1a, 2, 3b, &c., indicate dioptric, or lens lights, the figure showing the order or size, 1st, 2nd, 3rd, to 6th order ... b, a revolving lenticular light" [read, F87 text layer and F79 OCR]. So in 1887 the light was listed as a **first-order dioptric revolving** light. It was not a catoptric (reflector) light, which Findlay marks with a dot (Burry Port and the light-vessels on the same page carry the dot [seen]).
- **1839-1858: catoptric.** The 1859 keeper spoke of "eight reflectors and ... an equal number of lamps which were formerly used at this station" [read, RC61]. HAGUE: "The original Argand lamps were removed in 1858" [read].
- **From 1858: part of the old Lundy lens.** RC61 [read]: "The lens is a part only of the old lens from Lundy, and was placed a year ago instead of a reflecting light. There is only the central band of lenses with eight faces; there are no prisms above or below, and the greater part of the light is thus allowed to escape. The lens is composed of many small pieces cemented together; the joints were very evident ... cannot properly be called 'first order' dioptric." And "one large lamp" [read]; HAGUE: "lit by a four-burner lamp" [read]. TH61 records the cost of the swap [read]. Lundy's lens it came from was the 1842 Cookson optic, replaced at Lundy in 1857 (Wikipedia, "Lighthouses on Lundy" [read, secondary]).
- **1859 to 1887: no record found of any change.** Findlay lists 1b, 144 ft, 18 miles in both 1879 and 1887. HAGUE notes the service room "was strengthened with rolled-steel joists when a revolving apparatus was fitted, together with a central weight tube extending the full height of the tower" (CADW: "A cast-iron tube for the former clockwork weights runs its full height") [read], **undated**. The optic was replaced at electrification in 1959 and again in the 1970s (CADW, HAGUE) [read].
- **What the film shows: an eight-faced annular lens band, revolving, no prisms above or below** [inferred: the last description found (1859) plus Findlay's unchanged entry; a change between 1859 and 1887 can't be ruled out].
- **So: eight beams, and the lens turns once in 160 s, not once in 20 s** [inferred]. A revolving lens of N identical faces gives one flash per face; a flash every 20 s from eight faces means one turn in 8 x 20 = 160 s. One beam turning once in 20 s (the contract's first assumption) would need a one-faced lens, which no first-order revolving apparatus was. Each ship still sees a flash every 20 s, so the recorded rhythm is unchanged. In the close shots the difference is large: eight spokes of light round the lantern, 45 degrees apart, turning slowly (2.25 degrees a second).
- **Direction of rotation: not recorded** [chosen: clockwise seen from above, as the contract says].
- **Flash length, about 2.4 s** [inferred]. Beam divergence is about flame diameter / focal length. The largest wick of a four-wick first-order burner was 3.39 in (8.6 cm) across (Findlay 1887 intro on the first-order lamp [read]); the first-order focal length is 920 mm [read, the standard figure, not specific to this lens]. 0.086 / 0.92 = 0.094 rad = 5.4 degrees full width; at 360/160 = 2.25 degrees a second, a beam takes 2.4 s to pass. The cemented, joint-riddled 1842 lens will have spread it somewhat more; the film uses 5.4 degrees for the core with soft wings.
- **The escaping light** [inferred]. With no prisms above or below the band, the light from the flame outside the band's angular height leaves uncollimated ("the greater part of the light is thus allowed to escape", RC61). A first-order band about 0.9 m tall at 0.92 m focal distance covers about +/-26 degrees of elevation. Going up, light between about 26 and 45 degrees of elevation escapes through the upper glazing as a pale ring into the mist above the lantern, and the rest strikes the roof from inside. Going down, it hits the lantern floor and the iron lower wall (with the geometry below), so the rock under the light is not lit by it. The film shows the escaping ring as a dim upward glow; its strength is chosen.
- **Fog signal, 1879: "A Gong in fogs. Fog-trumpet proposed"** [read, F79]; 1859: "He has a gong" [read, RC61]. Not shown.

## The lantern

All from HAGUE [read] unless marked.

- Iron, inscribed "WILKINSON, LONG ACRE LONDON 1838"; "the oldest completely unaltered lantern in a working lighthouse in England and Wales".
- **Diameter 4.28 m (14 ft); glazed part 3.05 m (10 ft) high.**
- **Glazing: four rows of 24 panes, each 0.74 m (2 ft 5 in) high by 0.53 m (1 ft 9 in) wide.** Check [inferred]: 4 x 0.74 = 2.96 m against 3.05 m glazed (the rest is bars); 24 x 0.53 = 12.7 m against a circumference of 13.4 m, leaving about 3 cm per astragal.
- **Astragals vertical, not helical** [inferred from rectangular panes in rows; PC shows a rectangular grid, seen]. The lantern is a 24-sided prism in plan.
- **Flat glass in 1887.** "The upper and lower rows are of flat glass, but the middle two rows have been replaced by curved panes to eliminate false flashes, a minor modification made by the early 1970s." So in 1887 all 96 panes were flat [inferred].
- **Roof: "its original conical roof with ball finial-vent"**; CADW: "conical roof with a cowl" [read]. PC shows a low cone and a ball with something thin above it (a vane or conductor; unresolved at 600 px) [seen].
- **Lower wall: iron, 1.37 m (4 ft 6 in) high, "retains the original brass vent covers".** CADW: "gridded glazing over a solid iron-plate base" [read].
- **Outer gallery level with the base of the lower wall**, 1.07 m above the service-room floor; its outer ring carried on brackets.
- **Colour of the lantern ironwork in 1887: not found.** PC (before 1906) shows the roof pale and the lower wall and cornice zone dark [seen, at low resolution]. [chosen: roof and lower wall pale (white lead), astragals dark.]

## The tower

- **Height: 36 ft** [seen, F87, "White tower, 36 ft. high"]; 11.3 m (37 ft) [read, HAGUE].
- **White** [seen, F87; PC]. CADW: "rendered granite rubble, whitewashed" [read].
- **Designed by James Walker**; superintendent of works W. D. Frazer, with George Burrell [read, HAGUE]. (The coordinator's "James Walker, 1839" is right.)
- "The circular tower is built of granite and is very slightly tapering and capped by a rather feeble corbelled cornice, which has most unusual locally-made iron railings instead of Walker's usual battlemented parapet. The profile of the railings is bellied out in early nineteenth-century style, but the single intermediate horizontal rail has a modern appearance" [read, HAGUE]. PC shows the bellied railing round the gallery [seen].
- **One round-headed window**, north-facing [read, HAGUE, CADW].
- **Diameter: not found.** [inferred from PC: the tower's width is about 1.35 times the lantern's at the top, so about 5.8 m under the cornice; I use 6.3 m at the base for "very slightly tapering"].
- **Not lived in**; stone geometric stair; entered from a corridor linking it to the dwellings at mezzanine level, under a "Tudor style" (four-centred) arch [read, HAGUE, CADW].

## The dwellings and the compound

- "The two original houses are symmetrically planned and are also built of granite" [read, HAGUE]. CADW: "originally a pair of matching keepers' houses", hipped slate roof with an axial chimney stack, whitewashed render, two storeys; the western house was lowered to one storey in the 1970s [read].
- **In 1887: a pair of two-storey houses under one hipped roof, east of the tower, joined to it by a corridor** [inferred: CADW puts the lowered western house next to the tower via the corridor; PH18 shows tower, low section, house, left to right]. PC shows a two-storey block with a hipped roof and a pair of chimneys on the ridge, and a lower range between it and the tower [seen]. Sizes [chosen from PC proportions]: block about 13 m by 8 m, eaves about 6 m.
- **Walled compound**, low limewashed rubble walls, part of Walker's design [read, CADW]. OSM's outline of the station is about 40 m by 34 m [seen, plotted].
- The 1859 keeper: "Spray occasionally strikes the lantern, and it has broken the lower windows of the dwelling-house. There is a patch of grass in front of the door; rain water is collected from the roof" [read, RC61].
- Landing places on the west and east; steps and paths lined with iron hand-rail posts [read, HAGUE]; PC shows steps cut up the rock with a rail [seen]. (RC61's "Descended the rock by a stair cut in it" closes the previous station's entry, not this one; not used.)

## The rock

- **"The summit of a barren lava rock about 244 m (800 ft) by 122 m (400 ft) and 30.5 m (100 ft) high"** [read, HAGUE].
- **Plan shape: OSM coastline** [seen]: the main rock is about 170 m east-west by 100 m north-south above the coastline, with intertidal ledges to the south-west and a separate tidal reef to the east; the station sits east of centre on the summit. Modern, but rock doesn't move in 140 years.
- **Profile**: PH18 shows a steep-sided rock with a long lower shoulder to one side and the station at the high end [seen]; PC shows rough, blocky, fissured rock [seen].
- **Consistency check** [inferred]: rock 30.5 m + tower 11.3 m + lower wall 1.37 m + flame about 0.9 m above the glazing's base = 44.1 m, against Findlay's 43.9 m above high water. The numbers agree to 0.2 m, so the film's stack is: summit at y = -13.4 m, gallery at y = -2.3 m, glazing from y = -0.93 m to +2.12 m, flame at 0.
- **Sea level**: the contract puts the sea at y = -43.9 (high water). The tide at 11 P.M. to 2.30 A.M. on 21-22 Oct 1887 was not computed; the range on this coast is several metres. [chosen: high water].

## The night (from the project, for completeness)

- "S.E., 4; O. M." [printed entry]: wind from the south-east, Beaufort force 4 (the reports' scale runs 0 to 12, notes/PILOT.md), 5.5-7.9 m/s; weather O (overcast) and M (mist) in Beaufort's letters. The film uses 6.7 m/s toward the north-west.
- Mist: Beaufort's "m" is not a visibility class. [chosen] An extinction of about 2e-3 per metre (Koschmieder visibility 3.9/sigma = 2 km), denser near the sea, so that a ship 20 km off sees nothing but the flash brightening the mist.

- **Sunrise, 22 Oct 1887, at the South Bishop: 06:57 GMT; civil twilight from 06:23; sunset on the 21st 17:15 GMT**
  [computed: skyfield with `sources/ephemeris/de440s.bsp`, almanac.dark_twilight_day at 51.8527 N, 5.4119 W]. Lights
  burned sunset to sunrise [the standing rule; not checked in a period Trinity House source], so at "daylight" before
  06:57 the light was still turning.

## What the light kit draws (film/engine/light.js, light.glsl)

| element | how | status |
|---|---|---|
| eight beams, one turn in 160 s, flash every 20 s | Gaussian cones, 5.4 deg FWHM, 0.38 m at the lens, flux conserved; 4% of each beam in a wide faint wing (an old cemented lens) | inferred; the wing's share chosen |
| the lens band | eight flat faces, apothem 0.92 m, 0.9 m tall, brass rings above and below; a face blazes when its flame image (8.6 x 11 cm) covers your direction; rings of 4.8 cm, each a little off focus, so the flash comes on ring by ring | from RC61 + standard first-order numbers; ring pattern reconstructed |
| light escaping above the band | half the flame's intensity between 26 and 45 deg up; it floods the lantern's ceiling (seen from below as a bright ellipse) and lights the mist above | inferred from "no prisms above or below"; strength chosen |
| the glazing | 4 rows x 24 flat panes on vertical astragals, r = 2.13 m; 1% of the light crossing a pane scattered (salt: "Spray occasionally strikes the lantern", RC61) | recorded (Hague) / chosen (1%) |
| roof, ball vent, lower wall, gallery, railing, tower, cornice | as in "The lantern" and "The tower" above | recorded in outline; diameters and colours chosen |
| houses, corridor, compound wall | a pair under one hipped slate roof, long axis on bearing 110 deg, ESE of the tower; wall on the OSM outline | outline recorded, placement chosen |
| the rock | OSM plan, 30.5 m at the station, steep jointed sides in soft 2.4 m ledges, white water at its foot | plan modern-recorded, relief reconstructed |
| the mist | 3D noise (features about 40, 13 and 4 m) drifting 6.7 m/s toward the N.W., thicker near the sea; mean extinction 1.5e-3 /m; phase 0.7 HG(0.85) + 0.3 HG(0.3) | wind recorded; everything else chosen, physically typical |
| the far light | the lens as a point of conserved energy when under 5 px; the multiply-scattered aureole, exp(-tau (1-g)) less the unscattered and once-scattered parts, width 0.022 sqrt(tau) | physics standard; widths chosen |
| the sea | a plane at high water; wave normals from the S.E.; glitter by a Beckmann microfacet law under the lamp (Cox-Munk slope for 6.7 m/s, rounded down) | reconstructed |
| ambient light from the lit mist | the flux scattered within ~100 m spread over a ~50 m sphere, doubled for multiple scattering | rough estimate |
| surfaces in the lantern | rolled off toward paper (L / (1 + L/6)) so the print's halation doesn't swallow the bars; the lens flash is left whole | a printing choice, not physics |
